Community Meals

Privacy Policy

Last updated September 9, 2026

Community Meals (“the app”) connects volunteers with food-relief organizations that cook and deliver meals. This policy explains what information the app collects, why, who can see it, how to turn each piece of it off, and how to have it deleted. Plain answers, no defined terms — if something here is vague, that is a bug in the writing and we want to hear about it.

Who is responsible for your information

The people who make the app are. Every account here is created by the volunteer it belongs to, and every field in it is one you typed or chose yourself — no coordinator can create a record about you, add you to an organization, or edit your details. So the responsibility is ours, not your organization’s, and every request in this policy comes to us at the address at the bottom of this page. You never have to work out which of two parties to ask.

One honest limit. Once a coordinator has legitimately seen something — your name and phone number on a roster, say — what they then do outside the app is between you and them. If they write your number down or text you, the app is no longer involved and cannot take that back. Your organization may also have its own volunteer paperwork and its own privacy practices, which this policy does not cover.

How the app collects information

Only one way: you give it to us, by typing it or choosing it. Three things follow from that, and each is a promise about what is actually in the app, not a statement of intent:

  • Nothing is gathered in the background. No usage tracking, no analytics events, no crash or performance reporting, no device fingerprinting. The app contains no analytics or advertising software of any kind, so there is nothing running that could quietly observe how you use it.
  • Nothing about you arrives from anyone else. No data brokers, no social networks, no purchased lists, no background-check services. Joining an organization does not import a record about you from anywhere — it creates a membership and nothing more.
  • Things that never leave your phone are not collected at all. Filtering runs by distance happens on your own device, and a shift added to your calendar lives in your calendar. Those are described below for completeness, but they are not sent to us.

Information the app collects

  • Account information. Email address and password, handled by our backend provider (Supabase) — the app itself never stores your password.
  • Profile information. Your first and last name, a phone number, and your age (kept as a birth year, and used only to confirm you are at least 13 and whether a parent or guardian needs to agree first). A profile photo is optional. A phone number is required — a coordinator staffing a run, or a driver coming to collect a dish you cooked, has to be able to reach you, and the app offers them a call or text link rather than showing them a way around it.
  • A parent or guardian’s details, for volunteers under 18. Their name, email address and phone number, given by the guardian themselves during the young volunteer’s setup, together with which of the five items they agreed to. It is used only to record and confirm that consent and to contact them about it, and for nothing else. It is kept for as long as the volunteer’s account and is deleted with it. When the volunteer turns 18 the consent stops applying and they answer for themselves.
  • Home address. Optional, and never shared by default. If you add one, the app also stores its map coordinates so distances can be worked out. It is used to sort and filter opportunities by distance on your own device. Two separate, individually optional settings can show it to another person; both are off unless you turn them on, and they are described under “Your home address” below.
  • Photos you upload. Documentation of a shift (cooking, packing, delivery) or a profile picture. Photos are stored in a private cloud bucket; every organization photo is tied to the shift it documents. Each photo is resized and re-encoded on your device before it is uploaded, and the app never reads or stores location information from a photo file.
  • Reports and blocks. If you report a photo, we store which photo, the reason you picked, any note you add, and that it was you — coordinators of that organization need to know who raised it. If you block someone, we store that too; it is visible only to you, never to them and never to a coordinator.
  • Participation records. Which shifts you sign up for, cancel, or complete, and any note you add about your vehicle; supply items and dishes you claim, whether you have bought a supply item, and any note you add when you claim one (for example, how many people you are cooking for); if you join someone else’s dish to cook it together, that you joined it and when; which of those a coordinator has assigned you to drive; whether you have signalled interest in a role that is currently full; whether you have pledged to cover a run yourself if nobody else signs up, and any note with that pledge; and notifications about shifts you are on.

What the app uses it for

Every field above exists for one of these purposes, and is used for nothing else:

  • Signing you in and keeping your account yours.
  • Staffing and running shifts — showing a coordinator who has claimed what, and letting the people on a run recognise and reach each other.
  • Showing you opportunities near you, and helping a coordinator see which volunteers are close to a site.
  • Recording what a run cooked and delivered, and your own volunteer hours.
  • Keeping the app safe — handling reports of photos, and honouring the people you block.
  • Telling you when something about a shift you are on changes.

If we ever wanted to use something for a new purpose that is not on this list, we would ask you first.

What the app does not do

  • You are not tracked. Nothing about you is combined with data from other companies’ apps or websites, and nothing is shared with a data broker. That is Apple’s definition of tracking, and the app does none of it — which is why it never asks for permission to track you.
  • No advertising of any kind, and no advertising identifier is collected.
  • No analytics or crash-reporting software is built into the app.
  • No data is sold, and none is shared for targeted advertising, ever.
  • No data is used to train artificial-intelligence or machine-learning models, by us or by anyone else, and nothing is sent to an AI service.
  • No live location is read. The app never asks your phone where it is; the one location permission it requests exists only to turn an address you typed into coordinates.
  • Your contacts, address book, and other apps are never looked at.
  • Every image URL is a private, time-limited link — nothing is publicly hosted.

What stays on your device

A few small things are kept on your phone rather than sent to us, and none of them is visible to anyone else:

  • Your sign-in session, so you are not asked to sign in again every time you open the app.
  • Two small settings — whether device notifications are switched on, and whether you have dismissed the prompt that offers them.
  • Calendar entries you chose to add. If you add a shift to your phone’s calendar, that entry belongs to your calendar. The app stores only the entry’s ID, so it can remove the same entry later if you cancel.

In the browser version of the app these live in your browser’s local storage. The app sets no advertising or tracking cookies anywhere. Signing out, deleting the app, or clearing its data removes all of it.

Who can see your information

The app is organized around volunteer organizations. If you join one, the following is visible to other members of that same organization — this is how a coordinator finds people to staff a shift:

  • Your name, phone number, and profile photo.
  • Which shifts you have signed up for, and any note you added about your vehicle.
  • Which dish or supply item you have claimed, any note you added with it, who else has joined you to cook a dish together, and — if a coordinator has assigned someone to pick it up — who that is.
  • Which full roles you have signalled interest in, and any run you have pledged to cover yourself, along with any note on that pledge.
  • Your approximate distance from a site, rounded, only if a coordinator looks up “who’s nearby” — never your street address unless you turned on one of the two address settings described below.
  • For a volunteer under 18, a coordinator of their organization is also shown a short status on the roster: whether a guardian’s consent is still outstanding, whether a guardian is expected to accompany them, or that photographs of them are not to be uploaded. A guardian’s own name, email address and phone number are shown to no other volunteer or coordinator, ever.

Your profile record also carries your email address and the birth year worked out from the age you entered. No screen in the app shows either of these to anyone but you, but the database’s access rules protect whole records rather than individual fields, so another member of your organization who queried the data directly could retrieve them. We would rather say so than imply a protection that is not there.

Every volunteer is shown by the name they entered, whatever their age, everywhere a name appears to fellow volunteers — such as the roster for a shift. Names are only ever visible to people in the same organization as you.

Members of a different organization on the same app never see your information. Organizations do not share rosters with each other.

Your home address

An address is the most sensitive thing this app holds, so it is kept in its own place, with its own rules, and it is never shared as a side effect of anything else. Saving an address is not consent to show it to anybody. Two separate settings can share it, each off until you turn it on, each covering only what it says:

  • “Let coordinators see the address itself” (Profile → Where you are). With it off — the default — a coordinator of your organization can see only how far you are from a site, rounded, and never the address. With it on, coordinators of your organization can also see the address itself. Other volunteers cannot, either way.
  • Sharing a pickup address for one dish you claimed. When you claim a dish to cook at home and a coordinator assigns a driver to collect it, you can choose to show that one driver your saved address so they can find you. It applies to that single claim and to whoever is assigned to it at the time: if the claim is reassigned or the driver is removed, their access ends immediately, with nothing for you to switch off. If you do not turn it on, the driver gets your phone number and arranges the pickup with you instead.

Turning either setting off, or clearing the address field entirely, takes effect at once. Distances shown to a coordinator are rounded, which reduces but does not completely eliminate what can be inferred about where you live — that is the honest trade for the feature existing at all, and it is why the address itself stays behind a separate switch.

When the app hands you to another app

Some things the app offers are finished somewhere else, by an app you already have. Each of these is something you tap, never something that happens on its own:

  • Directions. Tapping a route opens Apple Maps or Google Maps — your choice — with the address being navigated to.
  • Calling or texting. Tapping a phone number opens your own phone or messages app with the number filled in. The app never sends a text or places a call itself, never sends you marketing messages, and never sees what you write.
  • Sharing a run. Tapping share opens your device’s own share sheet; you pick the app and the person, and the app only supplies the text and a link.

Once you are in one of those apps, that app’s own privacy policy applies and this one no longer does. We have no control over them and receive nothing back from them.

Other companies involved

Four, and no others. None of them is an advertising network, an analytics service, or an AI provider. Each is required to protect your information to at least the standard this policy describes, may use it only to perform the service it provides to the app, and may not sell it or use it for its own purposes.

  • Supabase hosts the database, the file storage, and the sign-in system this app runs on. It processes everything described above on the app’s behalf, and it is the only company that holds your data at rest.
  • Your phone’s own geocoder (Apple on iPhone, Google on Android) converts an address you save into coordinates. The address text is sent to that service by your device at the moment you save it. If you never save an address, this never happens.
  • OpenStreetMap’s Nominatim search service is used only when a coordinator looks up a kitchen or drop-off site by name while creating a run. It receives the search text the coordinator typed and nothing else — never a volunteer’s name, address, or any other personal information.
  • CARTO supplies the background map images on the Map tab. When you open that tab, your device asks CARTO for the map squares it needs to draw the area you are looking at, which tells CARTO which part of the map you are viewing. It is sent no name, no account, and no home address — and if you never open the Map tab, it is never contacted. The map software itself is built into the app rather than downloaded, so those map images are the only thing fetched.

Where your information is stored, and how it is protected

Everything is stored in the United States, in Supabase’s Oregon region (US-West-2). If you use the app from outside the United States, your information is sent to and stored in the United States, where privacy law may differ from your own country’s.

Every table is protected by row-level security rules enforced by the database itself, not just by the app’s screens, so a request for data you are not entitled to is rejected at the source rather than merely hidden from view. All traffic between the app and Supabase is encrypted in transit. Photos live in a private bucket and are only ever served through links that expire after an hour.

While the app is open, it holds a live connection to Supabase so that changes in your organization — a shift filling up, a delivery confirmed — reach your screen without you pulling to refresh. That connection carries only the same organization data you are already entitled to see.

If something goes wrong

No system is perfectly secure, and we would rather say that than imply otherwise. The protections above are real and are enforced by the database rather than by good intentions, but no one can promise that information sent over the internet is safe forever.

If we discover a breach that affects your information, we will email the people affected as soon as we reasonably can, tell you plainly what happened and what to do about it, and notify regulators where the law requires it. You will not learn about it from someone else first.

How long information is kept

  • Everything is kept for as long as your account exists. Your profile, participation records, photos you uploaded, and your notification history stay until you delete your account, which you can do at any time — see below.
  • Links to photos expire, the photos do not. Every image is served through a private link that stops working after an hour; the app generates a fresh one each time you view the photo.
  • Your notification feed shows recent items. Notifications you have already read drop out of the feed after about 90 days; unread ones stay until you read them.

Turning things off, and taking consent back

Nothing here requires you to contact anyone. Each of these takes effect immediately, from inside the app or your device’s own settings:

  • Your address. Profile → Where you are. Turn off “Let coordinators see the address itself,” or clear the field to remove the address altogether. Turn off the per-dish pickup sharing on the claim it belongs to.
  • Device notifications. Profile → the notifications card, which turns them back off as well as on.
  • Calendar, camera, photo library, and location permissions. Your device’s Settings app, under Community Meals. The app keeps working with any of them refused — an address can be typed without location access, and a shift can be worked without a calendar entry. Nothing is withheld from you for saying no.
  • Your photo, name, or phone number. Editable in Profile at any time. A phone number is required for as long as you hold an account; if you no longer want it on file, deleting your account removes it.
  • Everything at once. Delete your account, below. That is the complete withdrawal of consent, and it needs no one’s approval.

Deleting your account

You can permanently delete your account from inside the app at any time — Profile → Danger zone → Delete my account. This immediately and permanently removes your profile, phone number, home address and its sharing settings, your organization membership, your notifications, your saved photo, every shift sign-up you hold, every dish and supply claim, your place on any dish you joined to cook with someone else, every interest signal and backstop pledge, and the reports and blocks you created. There is no recovery period and no way to undo it.

Two categories of information intentionally survive, with your name removed from them, so that an organization’s operating history stays accurate for everyone else who relied on it: photos you contributed to a completed shift (now unattributed), and the record that a shift was delivered and by whom it was confirmed (also unattributed if that was you). If you were a coordinator, the runs you scheduled also remain, no longer attributed to you. Neither identifies you once your account is deleted.

If the app changes hands or shuts down

This app is not a data business, and your information is not an asset it would ever be sold for. Nobody will buy Community Meals to get a list of volunteers.

If the app is one day handed to someone else to run — most likely one of the organizations already using it — your information would move with it, this policy would continue to apply until you were told otherwise, and you would be emailed before the change with enough notice to delete your account first if you would rather not continue. If the app shuts down instead, accounts and their contents are deleted rather than sold, transferred, or left sitting on a server.

Your rights, and how to use them

Depending on where you live, privacy law may give you rights over your information. We honour these for everyone who asks, wherever they live, rather than checking your state first:

  • Know and see it. Most of it is on your own screens already. For a full copy, including anything the app does not display, email us and we will send it in a portable format.
  • Correct it. Your name, phone number, photo, and address are editable in Profile at any time. Email us for anything else.
  • Delete it. Profile → Danger zone → Delete my account, as described above. You do not need to ask us.
  • Opt out of sale, targeted advertising, and profiling. There is nothing to opt out of: the app does none of these, so a browser or device opt-out preference signal has nothing here to act on.

Email irehan29@icloud.com to make a request. We will respond within 45 days. If we refuse a request, our answer will say why, and you may appeal it by replying within 60 days — an appeal gets a written decision within 60 days of receiving it. If we deny your appeal you may complain to your state attorney general. We will never charge you for a request, and never treat you differently for making one.

Age, and volunteers under 18

The app requires every volunteer to be at least 13 years old, asked during sign-up, and does not knowingly collect information from anyone younger. If we learn that an account belongs to someone under 13, we delete it. A parent or guardian who believes their child under 13 has an account can email us and we will remove it.

A volunteer aged 13 to 17 needs a parent or guardian to agree before they can take part. That happens in the app, during their setup, with the adult present: the app asks for the guardian’s name, email address and phone number, and records what they agreed to, when, and which version of this policy and of the Community Guidelines was in force at the time.

The guardian answers five things separately, rather than agreeing to one combined statement. Two are required, because without them there is nothing to take part in: that their child may have an account and volunteer, and that their child’s full name is visible to other volunteers in their organization. The other three are genuine choices, and saying no to any of them does not stop the young person volunteering: whether photos taken at events that may include their child can be seen by others in the organization, whether a home address may be saved to show them the closest runs first, and whether their child may travel to a site without them.

  • Saying no to photos means that volunteer cannot upload any. This applies to their own uploads; the app does not attempt to identify anyone in photographs taken by other people.
  • Saying no to a home address means no address is saved for them, and the step asking for one is skipped.
  • Saying no to travelling alone means the coordinator running a shift is shown that a guardian is expected to come too. This is information for the coordinator, not something the app can check — the app has no way of knowing who actually arrives.

Until a guardian has agreed, the young volunteer can sign in, join an organization and look at what it is doing, but cannot sign up for a shift or upload a photo. The app says so on their screen rather than letting them try and be refused.

If this policy or the Community Guidelines are materially rewritten, consent given against the older wording stops counting and the guardian is asked again, rather than the old agreement being carried forward onto new terms. Consent also stops applying when the volunteer turns 18, at which point they answer for themselves.

A guardian can ask at any time to see what they agreed to, withdraw any single item, withdraw altogether, or have the account deleted, by emailing irehan29@icloud.com. There is a page written for them in the app, For parents and guardians, which can be opened without signing in.

No volunteer’s information, minor or adult, is ever used for advertising, profiling, or any purpose beyond the ones listed in this policy. Volunteering with an organization usually involves that organization’s own paperwork for minors, which is between you and them — this policy covers only the app.

Changes to this policy

If this policy changes in a way that affects what is collected or who can see it, the “Last updated” date at the top of this page will change accordingly, and we will tell you in the app before the change takes effect rather than expecting you to notice.
